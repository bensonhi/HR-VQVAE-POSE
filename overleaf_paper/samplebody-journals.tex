\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Co-speech gesture generation.}
\citet{mcneill1992hand} established the foundational taxonomy of
co-speech gestures, distinguishing \emph{beat} gestures---rhythmic
movements that emphasize speech prosody without semantic content---from
\emph{semantic} (iconic/metaphoric) gestures that visually represent
meaning.
This two-class distinction is reflected in the BEAT2 corpus~\cite{emage2024}
and drives the conditioning in our model.
Early data-driven approaches learn mappings from audio features to
skeletal joint rotations using RNNs or CNNs, typically restricted to
upper-body or hand-only motion~\cite{habibie,trimodal,ha2g}.
ZeroEGGS~\cite{zeroeggs2023} introduces example-based style control
through a learned style embedding, demonstrating that VAE-style latent
representations enable speaker-stylized gesture generation, although
it operates on a custom skeleton rather than the SMPL-X format used
in our setting.
Recent work has scaled to full-body SMPL-X generation.
TalkSHOW~\cite{talkshow2023} generates holistic body motion from
speech using a VQ-VAE stage followed by a Transformer prior, but
generates fixed-length clips without an explicit mechanism for
long-form continuity.
DiffuseStyleGesture~\cite{diffusestylegesture2023} runs a diffusion
model directly in continuous pose space with classifier-free guidance,
producing stylized output but relying on initial-frame conditioning
rather than a learned boundary objective for chunk-to-chunk continuity.
EMAGE~\cite{emage2024} introduces masked audio-gesture modeling for
holistic co-speech synthesis, alongside the BEAT2 (BEAT-SMPLX-FLAME)
corpus, establishing strong evaluation baselines for full-body gesture
quality.
MECo~\cite{meco2025}, concurrent work presented at SIGGRAPH 2025,
fine-tunes a large language model to interpret motion examples and
emit gesture tokens directly; both methods leverage LLMs in the
gesture pipeline, but MECo predicts motion tokens auto-regressively
from a residual VQ codebook whereas our planner outputs a high-level
gesture-type segmentation that drives a continuous-latent VAE,
side-stepping quantization entirely.
Furthermore, all prior methods that report results on BEAT2 do so
on a \emph{single speaker} (Speaker~2), leaving generalization across
the full 25-speaker corpus unverified.
Our work differs from all of the above in four ways:
(i)~a continuous hierarchical VAE latent aligned to anatomical body
parts replaces discrete codebooks;
(ii)~anchor-prefix conditioning with a continuation velocity loss
enables seamless arbitrary-length autoregression;
(iii)~an LLM planner predicts gesture-type labels from raw transcripts,
removing the dependency on dataset-specific annotations at inference
time; and
(iv)~the model is trained and evaluated on \emph{all 25 speakers},
demonstrating cross-speaker generalization that no prior work has
verified at this scale.

\paragraph{Hierarchical latent variable models.}
VQ-VAE-2~\cite{vqvae2} introduced a two-level hierarchy of discrete
codebooks for high-fidelity image generation, demonstrating that
coarse-to-fine factorization substantially improves reconstruction
quality over flat codebooks.
\citet{adiban2022} extended this idea with \emph{residual} vector
quantization across $L$ levels, where each level quantizes the
residual of the previous, yielding an $\mathcal{O}(L\!\cdot\!m)$
codebook search at decoding time despite an effective codebook size
of $m^L$.
Their HR-VQVAE serves as the direct precursor to our architecture.
However, discrete codebooks are a natural fit for image pixels,
which occupy a bounded, perceptually discrete space: nearby pixel
values that map to the same codeword look nearly identical to the
human eye.
SMPL-X pose parameters, by contrast, live in a continuous,
high-dimensional Lie group where even small quantization errors
compound across the kinematic chain and produce visible joint
snapping or unnatural pauses in motion.
We therefore inherit the hierarchical coarse-to-fine inductive bias
of HR-VQVAE but replace discrete quantization with a continuous
Gaussian latent, partitioning it channel-wise so that each slice is
supervised on a distinct body part (face, body, hands).
This design choice is the key reason our method produces smoother
trajectories than VQ-VAE-based gesture systems, as we verify
quantitatively in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.
On the generative model side, \citet{petrovich2021actor} proposed
a Transformer VAE for action-conditioned motion synthesis,
demonstrating that Transformer encoder-decoders generalize naturally
to variable-length sequences without architectural modifications —
a property we rely on to handle the variable-duration chunks in
our autoregressive pipeline.

\paragraph{Flow matching and diffusion priors over latents.}
Latent diffusion and flow-matching models have emerged as powerful
conditional priors over continuous representations.
\citet{lipman2023flow} formalized flow matching as a simulation-free
training objective for continuous normalizing flows, and
\citet{rectifiedflow} showed that straight-line rectified flows admit
exact integration and yield high-quality samples in very few ODE
steps.
We adopt the rectified-flow formulation to train a Transformer
velocity field over our temporal latent sequence, conditioned on
audio, speaker, and gesture-type context.
Unlike approaches that run diffusion directly in pose space, operating
in the compressed latent space is orders of magnitude cheaper per
inference step and decouples the prior's capacity from the output
resolution.

\paragraph{LLM-driven motion planning.}
Large language models have recently been applied to motion-related
planning tasks, exploiting their world knowledge and instruction
following to bridge natural language and motion primitives.
MECo~\cite{meco2025} fine-tunes an LLM to consume motion-example
prompts together with audio and emit gesture tokens directly.
Our planner differs in scope and design: rather than selecting
examples, it segments a timestamped transcript into gesture-type
spans (beat vs.\ semantic), producing a structured plan that drives
the downstream VAE directly.
We fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B~\cite{qwen3} with LoRA~\cite{lora2022}
on BEAT2 training data so that the planner learns the dataset's
boundary granularity and gesture-type distribution, eliminating the
need for manual \texttt{.sem} annotation at inference time.

We present a system for audio-driven co-speech gesture synthesis
that generates continuous, full-body SMPL-X motion
from speech audio and a speaker identity.
The system consists of three learned components
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}), trained sequentially:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a \textbf{body-part-hierarchical temporal VAE} trained progressively
      on all 25 speakers, encoding SMPL-X pose sequences into a
      continuous latent space structured around the body's anatomical
      hierarchy (Section~\ref{sec:vae});
\item a \textbf{conditional flow-matching prior} that first pretrains on
      all speakers and is then fine-tuned end-to-end with perceptual losses,
      sampling the latent conditioned on audio, speaker, and gesture type,
      combined with \textbf{anchor-prefix conditioning} for seamless
      long-form autoregression (Sections~\ref{sec:prior}
      and~\ref{sec:autoreg});
\item a \textbf{fine-tuned LLM gesture-type planner} that produces
      per-segment gesture-type annotations from raw speech transcripts,
      enabling operation on arbitrary recordings without
      dataset-specific labeling (Section~\ref{sec:planner}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Notation.}
Let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 165}$ denote a motion sequence of
$T$ frames at 30\,fps, represented as SMPL-X axis-angle pose vectors.
The 165 dimensions decompose into three anatomical parts:
body $\mathbf{x}_\text{B} \!=\! \mathbf{x}_{0:66}$,
face $\mathbf{x}_\text{F} \!=\! \mathbf{x}_{66:75}$, and
hands $\mathbf{x}_\text{H} \!=\! \mathbf{x}_{75:165}$.
Audio is represented by Wav2Vec~2.0 features
$\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 768}$~\cite{wav2vec2}.
Each motion segment is annotated with a discrete gesture type
$g \in \{\text{beat},\, \text{semantic}\}$~\cite{mcneill1992hand}
and a speaker identity $s$.
Anchor frames $\mathbf{x}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 165}$ denote the last
$K$ frames of the preceding segment, passed to the decoder during
autoregressive generation.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Body-Part-Hierarchical Temporal VAE}
\label{sec:vae}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Whereas \citet{adiban2022} hierarchically factorize a codebook through
residual vector quantization, we factorize a \emph{continuous} Gaussian
latent into $L\!=\!3$ ordered slices, each assigned to a distinct body part:
face ($\ell\!=\!0$), body ($\ell\!=\!1$), and hands ($\ell\!=\!2$).
The slice ordering imposes a coarse-to-fine inductive bias—face and body
motions are more global and easier to learn, while fine finger articulation
in the hand slice is activated last during progressive training.
All level losses backpropagate jointly through the shared encoder and
decoder weights in a single backward pass; the body-part assignment
ensures that each level's local loss signal is computed only from the
joints it is responsible for, so the dominant body-motion signal does
not suppress the weaker gradients from face and hand joints.

Although VQ-VAE variants dominate recent gesture generation
literature~\cite{adiban2022}, vector quantization introduces a
discretization step that inherently limits the smoothness of the
decoded motion: nearby poses that map to the same codebook entry
are reconstructed identically, producing subtle but perceptible
snapping artifacts in continuous trajectories.
By retaining a fully continuous Gaussian latent, our VAE avoids
quantization error entirely, yielding noticeably smoother joint
trajectories—an advantage we verify quantitatively through velocity
smoothness metrics in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\paragraph{Encoder.}
A Transformer encoder with sinusoidal positional encoding takes
$\mathbf{x}$ as input, with audio $\mathbf{a}$ added element-wise to the
frame embeddings before the first layer.
Speaker embedding $\mathbf{e}_s$ and gesture-type embedding $\mathbf{e}_g$
are added to every token.
A strided 1-D convolution (stride $r\!=\!8$) temporally downsamples the
encoder output, producing latent tokens
$\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times d}$ with $T' \!=\! \lceil T/r \rceil$.
Each token is projected to mean $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$
and log-variance $\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$
($d_z = 16$ in our experiments), and $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times d_z}$
is sampled via the reparameterization trick.
We choose a Transformer backbone specifically because its
self-attention mechanism operates on sequences of arbitrary length
without architectural changes, allowing the model to encode and
decode motion clips of any duration at training and inference
time—a property that RNN or CNN alternatives do not provide without
fixed-length padding or windowing heuristics.

\paragraph{Hierarchical latent factorization.}
The latent $\mathbf{z}$ is partitioned channel-wise into $L$ equal
slices:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z} = \bigl[\mathbf{z}^{(0)},\, \mathbf{z}^{(1)},\,
  \mathbf{z}^{(2)}\bigr],
\quad \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times (d_z/L)},
\end{equation}
with level $\ell$ supervised on body part $\ell$ (face/body/hands).
The per-level KL divergence is computed independently over each slice:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{KL} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}
  \mathrm{KL}\!\left(
    \prod_{t} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^{(\ell)},\,
      \mathrm{diag}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^{(\ell)2}\right)
    \,\Big\|\,
    \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})
  \right).
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}
This slice-level KL decomposition lets the progressive training
curriculum (Section~\ref{sec:train}) activate one level at a time without
any interaction between the corresponding posterior estimates.

\paragraph{Decoder.}
A Transformer decoder generates the output sequence at the original
frame rate $T$.
Each decoder block (\texttt{TemporalLatent\-Decoder\-Block}) applies
two independent cross-attention layers:
the first attends to the upsampled latent token sequence~$\mathbf{z}$;
the second attends to the full-resolution audio~$\mathbf{a}$,
optionally augmented with BERT text features.
The latent $\mathbf{z}$ additionally modulates every LayerNorm in the block
via DiT-style~\cite{dit} Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN), with the
affine parameters initialized to zero so that the initial output is the
identity.
This design enforces a deliberate disentanglement: \emph{$\mathbf{z}$ controls
identity and style} (captured in a speaker-and-content-agnostic bottleneck),
while \emph{audio drives timing and rhythm} through cross-attention.
Removing this separation during preliminary experiments caused the decoder
to ignore $\mathbf{z}$ and regress to an audio-only solution, visibly
collapsing speaker diversity.

\subsection{Training Objective and Body-Part-Progressive Curriculum}
\label{sec:train}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Per-level body-part loss.}
For level $\ell \in \{0,1,2\}$, the decoder produces a partial output
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(\ell)}$ using only $[\mathbf{z}^{(0)}, \ldots, \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}]$
(higher slices are zeroed out).
This partial output is supervised on the body part assigned to level $\ell$
with four loss terms—axis-angle MSE, velocity MSE, SMPL-X mesh-vertex MSE,
and joint-position MSE—all computed only on the relevant joint/vertex subset:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(\ell)}_\text{local} =
  \mathcal{L}_\text{aa}^{(\ell)}
  + \mathcal{L}_\text{vel}^{(\ell)}
  + \mathcal{L}_\text{mesh}^{(\ell)}
  + \mathcal{L}_\text{joint}^{(\ell)}.
\end{equation}
Including mesh and joint losses in SMPL-X space prevents axis-angle
accumulation errors from causing visible end-effector drift in finger and
face joints.

\paragraph{Global loss.}
The final decoder output $\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(L-1)}$ is supervised on all
body parts jointly with the same four-term loss:
$\mathcal{L}_\text{global} = \mathcal{L}_\text{aa} + \mathcal{L}_\text{vel}
+ \mathcal{L}_\text{mesh} + \mathcal{L}_\text{joint}$.

\paragraph{Free-bit KL.}
To prevent posterior collapse, we apply a target-KL floor:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_\text{KL} = \max(\tau,\, \mathcal{L}_\text{KL}),
\end{equation}
where $\tau \!\geq\! 0$ is a minimum KL threshold.
Below $\tau$, the KL gradient is zero and only the reconstruction gradient
propagates, giving the encoder freedom to build up a meaningful posterior
without premature compression.

\paragraph{Total loss.}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} =
  \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1} w_\ell \,\mathcal{L}^{(\ell)}_\text{local}
  +\, w_\text{g}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{global}
  +\, \beta(\text{epoch})\,\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_\text{KL}
  +\, \lambda_\text{cont}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{cont},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_\text{cont}$ is the continuation velocity loss
defined in Section~\ref{sec:autoreg}, and
$\beta(\text{epoch})$ is linearly annealed from 0 to its maximum value
over the first 100 epochs.

\paragraph{Progressive curriculum.}
Levels are activated coarse-to-fine across epoch ranges.
In epoch range $[E_\ell, E_{\ell+1})$, only the partial outputs
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}, \ldots, \hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(\ell)}$ contribute to the
local loss, so the decoder first masters global body motion before
taking on fine hand articulation.
The global loss always includes all levels.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.}
$d_\text{model}\!=\!512$, $d_z\!=\!16$, $L\!=\!3$, 8 attention heads,
6 encoder and 6 decoder layers, $d_\text{ff}\!=\!2048$, dropout 0.1,
temporal stride $r\!=\!8$, 25 speakers, $K_\text{max}\!=\!90$ anchor frames.
Trained for 400 epochs, batch size 8, Adam with $\text{lr}\!=\!10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Anchor-Prefix Conditioning and Chunked Autoregressive Generation}
\label{sec:autoreg}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Long recordings cannot be synthesized in a single forward pass due to
memory and length constraints, and independently generated chunks would
produce visually jarring discontinuities at boundaries.
We address both issues jointly with \emph{anchor-prefix conditioning}
during training and a matching \emph{chunked autoregressive} scheme at
inference.

\paragraph{Anchor-prefix conditioning.}
The decoder receives the $K$ frames immediately preceding the current
chunk—the \emph{anchor prefix} $\mathbf{x}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 165}$—
together with their matching audio slice $\mathbf{a}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 768}$.
These are projected and prepended to the audio cross-attention memory,
so every decoder query can attend to the preceding motion context.
The decoder also reconstructs the anchor frames alongside the main
sequence; this auxiliary reconstruction loss anchors the boundary pose.

\paragraph{Anchor curriculum.}
To bridge the gap between training and inference, anchor length $K$ is sampled according to a three-way curriculum:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{60\%:} full anchor ($K\!=\!K_\text{max}$), exactly matching
      the inference setting;
\item \textbf{20\%:} no anchor ($K\!=\!0$), training an unconditional fallback;
\item \textbf{20\%:} random $K \in [1, K_\text{max}-1]$, building robustness
      to shorter prefixes and to the first chunk of a recording.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Continuation velocity loss.}
Standard velocity loss penalizes frame-to-frame jitter uniformly across
the sequence, but the perceptually critical region is the
anchor-to-generated \emph{boundary}.
We introduce a \emph{linearly decaying} continuation velocity loss that
concentrates its weight there.
Let $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i$ denote the $i$-th generated frame, $\mathbf{x}_i$
the corresponding ground-truth frame, and $\mathbf{x}_a^{(K)}$ the last
anchor frame.
Define per-frame velocity residuals:
\begin{align}
\hat{\Delta}_i &= \begin{cases}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 - \mathbf{x}_a^{(K)} & i = 0 \\
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i-1} & i > 0
\end{cases},
\quad
\Delta_i = \begin{cases}
  \mathbf{x}_0 - \mathbf{x}_a^{(K)} & i = 0 \\
  \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_{i-1} & i > 0.
\end{cases}
\end{align}
The continuation loss over the first $n$ frames is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{cont} =
  \frac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} w_i}
  \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} w_i \,\bigl\|\hat{\Delta}_i - \Delta_i\bigr\|^2,
\quad w_i = 1 - \tfrac{i}{n}.
\label{eq:cont}
\end{equation}
The weight $w_0 \!=\! 1$ applies the largest penalty to the boundary
velocity $(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 - \mathbf{x}_a^{(K)})$, which is the velocity
most likely to produce a visible pop; weights decay linearly so that frames
deeper into the chunk are gently nudged but not over-constrained.

\paragraph{Inference: chunked autoregressive generation.}
At inference time the recording is first segmented by the gesture-type
planner (Section~\ref{sec:planner}).
Each segment is split at gesture-type transitions and further divided into
chunks of at most 150 frames (5 seconds at 30\,fps), with a minimum
of 5 frames.
Chunks are generated left-to-right:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Sample latent $\mathbf{z}_n$ from the flow-matching prior
      (Section~\ref{sec:prior}) conditioned on the chunk's audio,
      speaker, and gesture type.
\item Build the anchor prefix from the previous chunk's last
      $K\!=\!90$ frames and their audio.
\item Decode chunk $n$ with the temporal VAE decoder.
\item Optionally apply a cosine cross-fade over the overlap and a
      Gaussian post-smooth limited to the first few blend frames to
      further suppress any residual seam artifact.
\end{enumerate}
The chunked autoregressive loop is illustrated by the bottom feedback
arrow in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}.

\subsection{Conditional Flow-Matching Latent Prior}
\label{sec:prior}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Sampling $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ ignores the
audio, speaker, and gesture-type context entirely.
We replace the standard Gaussian prior with a conditional
\emph{flow-matching prior}~\cite{rectifiedflow} that models
$p(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{a}, s, g)$.

\paragraph{Architecture.}
An \texttt{AudioEncoder} (Transformer with CLS token, speaker and
gesture-type embeddings) produces a fixed-size context vector $\mathbf{c}$.
A Transformer velocity field $v_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, \mathbf{c})$, where
$t \in [0,1]$ is the flow time, denoises the latent token sequence
$\mathbf{z}_{1:T'}$.

\paragraph{VAE decoder fine-tuning.}
The VAE is first trained on all 25 speakers.
For the Speaker~2 evaluation, we additionally fine-tune the decoder
on Speaker~2 data alone (encoder frozen), producing a speaker-adapted
variant used only in the Speaker~2 pipeline.

\paragraph{Stage~1: All-speaker pretraining.}
With the VAE frozen, we sample $\mathbf{z}^* \sim q_\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a},s,g)$
from the encoder posterior and optimize the rectified-flow objective:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{flow} =
  \mathbb{E}_{t, \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
  \bigl\| v_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, \mathbf{c})
  - (\mathbf{z}^* - \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) \bigr\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]$, $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$,
and $\mathbf{z}_t = (1-t)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} + t\mathbf{z}^*$.
The prior is trained on all 25 speakers jointly; the best checkpoint is
selected by autoregressive FGD on the \emph{validation} split.

\paragraph{Stage~2: End-to-end perceptual fine-tuning.}
Starting from the all-speaker prior checkpoint, we fine-tune the prior
using a combined perceptual loss computed \emph{through} the frozen VAE
decoder, which allows gradients from motion-quality objectives to shape
the prior's sampling distribution directly:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{e2e} =
  \mathcal{L}_\text{flow}
  + \lambda_\text{fgd}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{FGD}
  + \lambda_\text{beat}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{beat}
  + \lambda_\text{cov}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{cov},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_\text{FGD}$ is a feature-matching loss from the FGD
encoder, $\mathcal{L}_\text{beat}$ penalizes misalignment between motion
peaks and audio beat onsets, and $\mathcal{L}_\text{cov}$ encourages
diversity in the decoded motion.
Gradients flow through a small number of differentiable Euler steps of the
prior, then through the frozen decoder.
We use $\lambda_\text{fgd}\!=\!0.01$, $\lambda_\text{beat}\!=\!0.1$,
$\lambda_\text{cov}\!=\!1.0$, and $\text{lr}\!=\!10^{-5}$.

\paragraph{Sampling.}
Starting from $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$,
we integrate $v_\theta$ with Euler's method over $N\!=\!50$ steps to obtain $\mathbf{z}$,
which is then passed to the VAE decoder.
A guidance scale $g$ (selected on the validation set; $g\!=\!0.5$ in our
final evaluation) controls the trade-off between fidelity and diversity.

\subsection{LLM Gesture-Type Planner}
\label{sec:planner}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

The VAE decoder conditions on a gesture-type label $g$ that controls
whether the current segment should exhibit rhythmic beat gestures or
meaning-reinforcing semantic gestures.
At training time these labels come from BEAT2 \texttt{.sem}
annotations, which are dataset-specific and unavailable for arbitrary
speech.
We bridge this gap with a \emph{gesture-type planner}: a language model
fine-tuned to predict per-segment gesture types directly from a speech
transcript.

\paragraph{Input and output.}
The planner receives a word-level transcript with per-word timestamps
(extracted from a forced-alignment TextGrid file or an ASR system).
It outputs a JSON array of segments:
\begin{verbatim}
[{"start_sec": 0.0, "end_sec": 2.4,
  "gesture_type": "beat", "text": "..."},
  ...]
\end{verbatim}
This JSON is consumed directly by the chunk-segmentation step of the
autoregressive generation pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:autoreg}).

\paragraph{Model and fine-tuning.}
We fine-tune \textbf{Qwen3.5-9B}~\cite{qwen3} using LoRA via SFTTrainer
on the BEAT2 training split~\cite{emage2024}.
Each training example is a chat-format conversation:
a fixed system prompt describing the output schema, a user message
containing the word-timed transcript, and an assistant response
containing the ground-truth JSON segment plan.
The ground-truth plans are derived automatically from the BEAT2
\texttt{.sem} files, so the planner learns the BEAT2 gesture-type
vocabulary and boundary conventions without manual annotation.

\paragraph{Why fine-tune rather than zero-shot.}
Zero-shot prompting of a general LLM produces syntactically valid JSON
only inconsistently, and more importantly, it is uncalibrated to the
BEAT2 distribution of beat-vs-semantic proportions and boundary granularity.
Fine-tuning on BEAT2 training data closes both gaps.

\paragraph{Inference.}
At inference time, the planner runs once per recording, after which its
output drives the chunk-building and autoregressive generation steps
described in Section~\ref{sec:autoreg}, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:overview}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

% TODO: expand with dataset details, ablations, qualitative results.

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We use the BEAT2 (v2.0.0) English corpus~\cite{emage2024}, which contains
recordings from 25 speakers with per-frame SMPL-X pose annotations,
audio, transcripts, and gesture-type (\texttt{.sem}) labels.
We follow the official train/val/test split.
All prior methods that report results on BEAT2 evaluate on
\emph{Speaker~2 only}; we train a single model on \emph{all 25 speakers}
jointly and evaluate on both the Speaker~2 test set (for direct
comparison with baselines) and the full 25-speaker test set (to
verify cross-speaker generalization).

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
All hyperparameter and checkpoint selection decisions---including the
classifier-free guidance scale $g$---are made on the \emph{validation} split.
Final metrics are computed on the \emph{test} split with 5 random seeds;
we report mean~$\pm$~std.
BC values are reported in the scale used by prior work (raw metric $\times 10$).

\subsection{Quantitative Comparison}

We report three standard metrics:
\textbf{FGD} (Fr\'echet Gesture Distance~\cite{emage2024}; lower is
better), \textbf{BC} (Beat Consistency~\cite{emage2024}; $\rightarrow$
closer to GT is better), and \textbf{L1Div} (L1 diversity; higher is
better).
Our numbers are averaged over 5 random seeds; mean $\pm$ standard
deviation are reported.
Baselines are taken from the SynTalker comparison
table~\cite{syntalker} and evaluate on Speaker~2 only; ``---''
indicates the metric was not reported.

\paragraph{Speaker~2 comparison.}
Table~\ref{tab:results} compares our method against prior work on the
standard Speaker~2 test set.
Despite being trained on all 25 speakers simultaneously rather than
fine-tuned on Speaker~2 alone, our model achieves the best FGD and
L1Div, and a BC closest to the ground truth.

\paragraph{Cross-speaker generalization.}
Table~\ref{tab:results_allspk} reports results on the full 25-speaker
test set.
We compare against EMAGE~\cite{emage2024} and SynTalker~\cite{syntalker},
both re-trained from their official codebases on the full 25-speaker
training set with default hyperparameters and the same epoch budgets
used in their respective original papers.
For reference, the released EMAGE checkpoint
(trained on Speaker~2 only) also fails to generalize, scoring
FGD~$25.1$ on this test set; re-training on all speakers does not close
the gap, indicating that the limitation is architectural rather than
a matter of training-data coverage.
To further verify that the aggregate result is not masking collapse on
a subset of speakers, we evaluate the all-speaker prior (without any
speaker-specific fine-tuning) on each speaker individually.
Per-speaker FGD has median $8.86$ and ranges from $4.41$ on the easiest
speaker to $34.1$ on the hardest, with $24$ of $25$ speakers below FGD
$\approx\!18$.
The hardest speakers correspond to those with the most distinctive
motion styles in BEAT2.
Per-speaker fine-tuning further reduces error on individual speakers,
as demonstrated by the Speaker~2 results in Table~\ref{tab:results}
($\text{FGD} = 4.374$ for the spk2-fine-tuned model vs.\ $5.82$ for the
all-speaker model evaluated on Speaker~2 alone).

\paragraph{Anchor prefix.}
Removing anchor-prefix conditioning (\texttt{anchor\_k=0} at inference)
degrades FGD by $0.81$ and pushes BC further from ground truth.
This confirms that exposing the decoder to the previous chunk's motion
context is necessary for temporal coherence across autoregressive
boundaries.

\paragraph{Flow-matching prior.}
Replacing the learned prior with $\mathbf{z} \!\sim\! \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$
sampling causes the largest single drop in quality, increasing FGD by
$3.01$ and collapsing diversity (L1Div drops from $14.85$ to $9.94$).
BC counter-intuitively rises above ground truth, but this reflects
over-animated, audio-uncorrelated motion that registers spurious beat
hits rather than genuinely speech-aligned gesture.

\paragraph{End-to-end perceptual fine-tuning.}
Skipping Stage~2 of the prior (using the all-speaker pretrained prior
without speaker-specific perceptual fine-tuning) increases FGD by
$0.999$ on Speaker~2.
This isolates the contribution of the combined flow-matching, FGD
feature-matching, beat alignment, and covariance loss
(Section~\ref{sec:prior}) from that of all-speaker pretraining alone.

\paragraph{Continuation velocity loss.}
We isolate the continuation velocity loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:cont}) by
retraining the VAE with $\lambda_\text{cont} \!=\! 0$ (and the auxiliary
anchor reconstruction weight set to zero), keeping all other settings
identical.
Boundary metrics in Table~\ref{tab:cont_ablation} are computed in chunked
autoregressive \emph{reconstruction} mode (encode ground-truth chunks,
decode each chunk using the previous decoded chunk as anchor), so the
prior is not involved and the comparison isolates the decoder's
boundary behavior.
Removing the loss roughly \emph{doubles} the boundary velocity error
(33.4 vs.~16.6\,mm) and the boundary jerk (37.9 vs.~17.3\,mm), confirming
that the loss is the principal mechanism behind seamless chunk-to-chunk
transitions; within-chunk reconstruction quality also improves slightly
when the loss is enabled, suggesting it acts as a mild global regularizer
in addition to its boundary-specific role.

continuous tokenizer generalizes across the full corpus without per-speaker
