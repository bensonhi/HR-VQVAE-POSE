\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Believable digital characters are increasingly central to interactive
entertainment, embodied conversational agents, and immersive
telepresence.
A defining property of such characters is that the body
\emph{moves with speech}: face, body, and hands articulate in synchrony
with prosody and meaning, conveying intent that text alone cannot.
Generating such co-speech gestures automatically---given only audio,
without intervening motion capture---would lift a major bottleneck for
character animation pipelines, and is a long-standing problem in
computer graphics~\cite{mcneill1992hand,s2g,beat,emage2024}.

Recent years have seen rapid progress on this problem, driven by the
advent of mesh-level parametric body models such as
SMPL-X~\cite{smplx}---a unified body model whose 55 articulated
joints (body, hands, jaw, eyes) yield a $165$-dimensional axis-angle
pose vector, augmented with separate parameters for facial
expression---and the release of large-scale motion-captured gesture
corpora such as BEAT and BEAT2~\cite{beat,emage2024}.
On top of these substrates, the strongest current methods rely on a
common recipe: a vector-quantized autoencoder~\cite{vqvae,vqvae2}
that discretizes motion into codebook tokens, followed by an
autoregressive or diffusion-based prior over those tokens conditioned
on speech.
TalkSHOW~\cite{talkshow2023} pairs a compositional VQ-VAE for body and
hands with a Gated PixelCNN autoregressive prior; EMAGE~\cite{emage2024}
trains masked audio-gesture transformers over four compositional
VQ-VAE tokenizers; GestureDiffuCLIP~\cite{gesturediffuclip} runs latent
diffusion in a VQ-VAE-pretrained gesture latent space; and SynTalker~\cite{syntalker}
trains a residual VQ-VAE motion tokenizer and runs latent diffusion
in its latent space for prompt-conditioned full-body motion.
Outside the gesture domain, recent text-driven human motion synthesis
ranges from continuous-space diffusion such as
MDM~\cite{mdm} to VQ-based token generation, frequently trained on
HumanML3D~\cite{humanml3d}.

Three open problems still hold the field back.
First, \emph{quantization artifacts.}
SMPL-X pose lives in a continuous, high-dimensional space, where even
small errors propagate along the kinematic chain and produce visible
joint snapping or unnatural pauses.
Discrete codebooks are an awkward fit: codeword boundaries become
discontinuities in the decoded motion.
Second, \emph{long-form coherence.}
Training clips are typically short fixed-length windows, and the
resulting models do not natively scale to multi-minute speech.
Existing methods chain successive windows through seed-frame
conditioning or post-hoc smoothing, but none train the decoder
explicitly for kinematic continuity at chunk boundaries.
Third, \emph{cross-speaker generalization.}
Despite the BEAT2 corpus containing recordings from $25$ speakers, all
recent published numbers on this benchmark report results from a
\emph{single speaker} (Speaker~2)~\cite{emage2024,syntalker}, leaving
the field's claims about deployable gesture synthesis essentially
unverified across speaker styles.

We present \textbf{GestureCraft}, an audio-to-motion framework that
addresses all three problems within a single pipeline.
Our central design choice is to replace the discrete codebook with a
\emph{continuous, body-part-hierarchical} Gaussian latent: a
temporally downsampled sequence of $d_z\!=\!16$-dimensional tokens,
each partitioned channel-wise into face, body, and hand slices, with
per-level partial-output supervision that drives each slice to
specialize on its assigned body part, learned through a
Transformer~\cite{transformer} encoder-decoder VAE.
Long-form motion is supported by an \emph{anchor-prefix} conditioning
mechanism paired with a \emph{continuation velocity loss} that
explicitly trains the decoder for velocity continuity at chunk
boundaries.
A conditional flow-matching prior~\cite{lipman2023flow,rectifiedflow}
over the continuous latent is pretrained on all $25$ BEAT2 speakers and
fine-tuned end-to-end with perceptual losses---an FGD~\cite{trimodal,fid}
moment-matching term (mean and covariance in feature space) and a
beat-alignment term.
Finally, a LoRA-fine-tuned~\cite{lora2022} Qwen3.5-9B~\cite{qwen3}
gesture-type planner produces per-segment gesture-type
labels~\cite{mcneill1992hand} from raw transcripts, removing the
inference-time dependency on dataset-specific annotations.

We summarize our contributions as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{body-part-hierarchical continuous VAE} for SMPL-X
      gesture motion, replacing the discrete codebooks of prior
      methods with a channel-wise factorized Gaussian latent that
      reconstructs joint position with up to $2.8{\times}$ lower
      error than the released VQ tokenizers of two leading
      systems~\cite{emage2024,syntalker} on the standard Speaker~2
      benchmark and roughly $4{\times}$ lower error on the full
      $25$-speaker corpus.
\item \textbf{Anchor-prefix conditioning with a continuation velocity
      loss}, a learned objective that approximately halves boundary
      velocity error and boundary jerk relative to a no-continuation
      baseline, enabling seamless chunked autoregression at
      arbitrary recording lengths.
\item A \textbf{fine-tuned LLM gesture-type planner} that converts
      raw transcripts into BEAT2-style gesture-type segmentations,
      preserving downstream generation quality while removing the
      inference-time dependency on dataset-specific
      annotations.
\item A \textbf{cross-speaker generalization study} that, to our
      knowledge, is the first to report results on the full BEAT2
      25-speaker test set under the standard evaluation protocol;
      we outperform re-trained VQ baselines by close to an order of
      magnitude on FGD without sacrificing per-speaker quality on
      Speaker~2.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews prior work on co-speech gesture
generation, hierarchical latents, flow-matching priors, and LLM-driven
motion planning.
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the GestureCraft pipeline in
detail.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports quantitative comparisons,
cross-speaker generalization, ablations of each pipeline component,
and an evaluation of the LLM planner.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Co-speech gesture generation.}
\citet{mcneill1992hand} established the foundational taxonomy of
co-speech gestures, distinguishing \emph{beat} gestures---rhythmic
movements that emphasize speech prosody without semantic content---from
\emph{semantic} (iconic/metaphoric) gestures that visually represent
meaning.
This two-class distinction is reflected in the BEAT2 corpus~\cite{emage2024}
and drives the conditioning in our model.
Early data-driven approaches learn mappings from audio features to
skeletal joint rotations using RNNs or
CNNs~\cite{s2g,trimodal,ha2g,habibie}, with most methods restricted to
upper-body or hand-only motion.
ZeroEGGS~\cite{zeroeggs2023} introduces example-based style control
through a learned style embedding, demonstrating that VAE-style latent
representations enable speaker-stylized gesture generation, although
it operates on a custom skeleton rather than the SMPL-X format used
in our setting.
Recent work has scaled to full-body SMPL-X generation.
TalkSHOW~\cite{talkshow2023} generates holistic body motion from
speech using a compositional VQ-VAE for body and hand motion together
with a Gated PixelCNN autoregressive prior over codebook indices;
long-form quality depends on rollout stability, as there is no
explicit boundary-continuity objective.
DiffuseStyleGesture~\cite{diffusestylegesture2023} runs a diffusion
model directly in continuous pose space with classifier-free guidance,
producing stylized output but relying on initial-frame conditioning
rather than a learned boundary objective for chunk-to-chunk continuity.
GestureDiffuCLIP~\cite{gesturediffuclip} runs latent diffusion in
a VQ-VAE-pretrained gesture latent space with CLIP-based style control,
demonstrating that VQ-then-diffusion pipelines can produce stylized
co-speech motion.
EMAGE~\cite{emage2024} introduces masked audio-gesture modeling for
holistic co-speech synthesis, alongside the BEAT2 (BEAT-SMPLX-FLAME)
corpus, establishing strong evaluation baselines for full-body gesture
quality.
MECo~\cite{meco2025}, concurrent work presented at SIGGRAPH 2025,
fine-tunes a large language model to interpret motion examples and
emit gesture tokens directly; both methods leverage LLMs in the
gesture pipeline, but MECo predicts motion tokens auto-regressively
from a residual VQ codebook whereas our planner outputs a high-level
gesture-type segmentation that drives a continuous-latent VAE,
side-stepping quantization entirely.
Furthermore, all prior methods that report results on BEAT2 do so
on a \emph{single speaker} (Speaker~2), leaving generalization across
the full 25-speaker corpus unverified.
Our work differs from all of the above in four ways:
(i)~a continuous hierarchical VAE latent aligned to anatomical body
parts replaces discrete codebooks;
(ii)~anchor-prefix conditioning with a continuation velocity loss
enables seamless arbitrary-length autoregression;
(iii)~an LLM planner predicts gesture-type labels from raw transcripts,
removing the dependency on dataset-specific annotations at inference
time; and
(iv)~the model is trained and evaluated on \emph{all 25 speakers},
demonstrating cross-speaker generalization that no prior work has
verified at this scale.

\paragraph{Hierarchical latent variable models.}
VQ-VAE~\cite{vqvae} represents data via a learned discrete codebook
of latent indices, decoupling a powerful autoregressive prior from a
lightweight encoder/decoder.
VQ-VAE-2~\cite{vqvae2} introduced a multi-scale hierarchy of such
codebooks for high-fidelity image generation, demonstrating that
coarse-to-fine factorization substantially improves reconstruction
quality over flat codebooks.
\citet{adiban2022} extended this idea with \emph{residual} vector
quantization across $L$ levels, where each level quantizes the
residual of the previous, yielding an $\mathcal{O}(L\!\cdot\!m)$
codebook search at decoding time despite an effective codebook size
of $m^L$.
Their HR-VQVAE serves as the direct precursor to our architecture.
However, discrete codebooks are a natural fit for image pixels,
which occupy a bounded, perceptually discrete space: nearby pixel
values that map to the same codeword look nearly identical to the
human eye.
SMPL-X pose parameters, by contrast, live in a continuous,
high-dimensional Lie group where even small quantization errors
compound across the kinematic chain and produce visible joint
snapping or unnatural pauses in motion.
We therefore inherit the hierarchical coarse-to-fine inductive bias
of HR-VQVAE but replace discrete quantization with a continuous
Gaussian latent, partitioning it channel-wise so that each slice is
supervised on a distinct body part (face, body, hands).
The reconstruction comparisons in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} show that this continuous formulation
achieves substantially lower per-joint error than two representative
VQ tokenizers.
On the generative model side, \citet{petrovich2021actor} proposed
a Transformer~\cite{transformer} VAE for action-conditioned motion
synthesis, demonstrating that Transformer encoder-decoders generalize
naturally to variable-length sequences without architectural
modifications --- a property we rely on to handle the variable-duration
chunks in our autoregressive pipeline.
Beyond gesture, \citet{mdm} introduced a transformer-based diffusion
model for general human motion synthesis trained on the
HumanML3D~\cite{humanml3d} text-to-motion corpus, demonstrating the
viability of continuous-pose generative modeling at scale.

\paragraph{Flow matching and diffusion priors over latents.}
Building on denoising diffusion~\cite{ddpm}, latent diffusion and
flow-matching models have emerged as powerful conditional priors
over continuous representations.
\citet{lipman2023flow} formalized flow matching as a simulation-free
training objective for continuous normalizing flows, and
\citet{rectifiedflow} showed that straight-line rectified flows admit
exact integration and yield high-quality samples in very few ODE
steps.
We adopt the rectified-flow formulation to train a Transformer
velocity field over our temporal latent sequence, conditioned on
audio, speaker, and gesture-type context.
Unlike approaches that run diffusion directly in pose space,
operating in the compressed latent space (temporal stride $r\!=\!8$,
$d_z\!=\!16$) reduces the cost of each ODE step and decouples the
prior's capacity from the output resolution.

\paragraph{LLM-driven motion planning.}
Large language models have recently been applied to motion-related
planning tasks, exploiting their world knowledge and instruction
following to bridge natural language and motion primitives.
MECo~\cite{meco2025} fine-tunes an LLM to consume motion-example
prompts together with audio and emit gesture tokens directly.
Our planner differs in scope and design: rather than selecting
examples, it segments a timestamped transcript into gesture-type
spans (beat vs.\ semantic), producing a structured plan that drives
the downstream VAE directly.
We fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B~\cite{qwen3} with LoRA~\cite{lora2022}
on BEAT2 training data so that the planner learns the dataset's
boundary granularity and gesture-type distribution, eliminating the
need for manual \texttt{.sem} annotation at inference time.

We present a system for audio-driven co-speech gesture synthesis
that generates continuous, full-body SMPL-X motion
from speech audio and a speaker identity.
The system consists of three learned components
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}), trained in stages:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a \textbf{body-part-hierarchical temporal VAE} trained on all 25
      speakers, encoding SMPL-X pose sequences into a continuous
      latent space structured around the body's anatomical hierarchy
      (Section~\ref{sec:vae});
\item a \textbf{conditional flow-matching prior} that first pretrains on
      all speakers and is then fine-tuned end-to-end with perceptual losses,
      sampling the latent conditioned on audio, speaker, and gesture type,
      combined with \textbf{anchor-prefix conditioning} for seamless
      long-form autoregression (Sections~\ref{sec:prior}
      and~\ref{sec:autoreg});
\item a \textbf{fine-tuned LLM gesture-type planner} that produces
      per-segment gesture-type annotations from raw speech transcripts,
      enabling operation on arbitrary recordings without
      dataset-specific labeling (Section~\ref{sec:planner}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Notation.}
Let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 165}$ denote a motion sequence of
$T$ frames at 30\,fps, represented as SMPL-X axis-angle pose vectors.
The 165 dimensions decompose into three anatomical parts:
body $\mathbf{x}_\text{B} \!=\! \mathbf{x}_{0:66}$,
face $\mathbf{x}_\text{F} \!=\! \mathbf{x}_{66:75}$, and
hands $\mathbf{x}_\text{H} \!=\! \mathbf{x}_{75:165}$.
Audio is represented by Wav2Vec~2.0 features
$\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 768}$~\cite{wav2vec2}.
Each motion segment is annotated with a discrete gesture type
$g \in \{\text{beat},\, \text{semantic}\}$~\cite{mcneill1992hand}
and a speaker identity $s$.
Anchor frames $\mathbf{x}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 165}$ denote the last
$K$ frames of the preceding segment, passed to the decoder during
autoregressive generation.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Body-Part-Hierarchical Temporal VAE}
\label{sec:vae}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Whereas \citet{adiban2022} hierarchically factorize a codebook through
residual vector quantization, we factorize a \emph{continuous} Gaussian
latent into $L\!=\!3$ ordered slices, each assigned to a distinct body part:
face ($\ell\!=\!0$), body ($\ell\!=\!1$), and hands ($\ell\!=\!2$).
The slice ordering reflects the relative dimensionality of each body
part: face (jaw + eyes) spans 9 scalar dimensions, body 66, and hands
90.
Through partial-output decoding (Section~\ref{sec:train}), level $0$
must reconstruct the face from $\mathbf{z}^{(0)}$ alone, level $1$
adds the body using $[\mathbf{z}^{(0)}, \mathbf{z}^{(1)}]$, and level
$2$ adds the hands using the full latent---so smaller body-part slices
specialize first along the channel-wise hierarchy.
All level losses backpropagate jointly through the shared encoder and
decoder weights in a single backward pass; the body-part assignment
ensures that each level's local loss signal is computed only from the
joints it is responsible for, so the dominant body-motion signal does
not suppress the weaker gradients from face and hand joints.

Although VQ-VAE variants dominate recent gesture generation
literature~\cite{adiban2022,emage2024,talkshow2023,syntalker,gesturediffuclip},
vector quantization introduces a discretization step that limits the
smoothness of the decoded motion: nearby poses that map to the same
codebook entry are reconstructed identically, producing velocity
discontinuities at codeword boundaries.
By retaining a fully continuous Gaussian latent, our VAE avoids this
quantization step.
The reconstruction comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} show
that this continuous formulation both reduces per-joint error and
produces measurably smoother decoded motion: HR-VQVAE attains
P99 jerk within $2.6{\times}$ of ground truth, against
$13.5{\times}$ for EMAGE's VQ tokenizer.

\paragraph{Encoder.}
A Transformer encoder with sinusoidal positional encoding takes
$\mathbf{x}$ as input, with audio $\mathbf{a}$ added element-wise to
the frame embeddings before the first layer.
After the transformer trunk, a speaker embedding $\mathbf{e}_s$ and a
gesture-type embedding $\mathbf{e}_g$ are broadcast-added across the
temporal dimension.
A strided 1-D convolution (stride $r\!=\!8$) temporally downsamples the
encoder output, producing latent tokens
$\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times d}$ with $T' \!=\! \lceil T/r \rceil$.
Each token is projected to mean $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$
and log-variance $\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$
($d_z = 16$ in our experiments), and $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times d_z}$
is sampled via the reparameterization trick.
We choose a Transformer backbone specifically because its
self-attention mechanism operates on sequences of arbitrary length
without architectural changes, allowing the model to encode and
decode motion clips of any duration at training and inference
time—a property that RNN or CNN alternatives do not provide without
fixed-length padding or windowing heuristics.

\paragraph{Hierarchical latent factorization.}
The latent $\mathbf{z}$ is partitioned channel-wise into $L$ ordered
slices:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z} = \bigl[\mathbf{z}^{(0)},\, \mathbf{z}^{(1)},\,
  \mathbf{z}^{(2)}\bigr],
\quad \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times d_\ell},
\end{equation}
with level $\ell$ supervised on body part $\ell$ (face/body/hands).
The slice widths $d_\ell$ partition $d_z$ as evenly as possible
($d_z\!=\!16$, $L\!=\!3$ gives $(5,5,6)$).
The per-level KL divergence is computed independently over each slice:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{KL} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}
  \mathrm{KL}\!\left(
    \prod_{t} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^{(\ell)},\,
      \mathrm{diag}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^{(\ell)2}\right)
    \,\Big\|\,
    \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})
  \right).
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}
This slice-level KL decomposition lets each level's per-part loss
(Section~\ref{sec:train}) regularize its assigned slice independently,
without coupling its posterior to the others through a shared KL
term.

\paragraph{Decoder.}
A pre-norm Transformer decoder generates the output sequence at the
original frame rate $T$.
Each decoder block applies self-attention followed by two independent
cross-attention layers:
the first attends to the temporal latent token sequence~$\mathbf{z}$,
to which the speaker embedding $\mathbf{e}_s$ and gesture-type
embedding $\mathbf{e}_g$ are added prior to positional encoding;
the second attends to the full-resolution audio~$\mathbf{a}$,
optionally augmented with BERT text features.
Splitting the conditioning across two memory streams allows the
decoder to separately attend to identity- and style-bearing
information in~$\mathbf{z}$ and to timing- and rhythm-bearing
information in the audio, while keeping both modalities available
at every layer.

\subsection{Training Objective}
\label{sec:train}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Per-level body-part loss.}
For level $\ell \in \{0,1,2\}$, the decoder produces a partial output
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(\ell)}$ using only
$[\mathbf{z}^{(0)}, \ldots, \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}]$ (higher slices are
zeroed out).
This partial output is supervised on the body part assigned to level
$\ell$ with four loss terms---axis-angle MSE, velocity MSE, SMPL-X
mesh-vertex MSE, and joint-position MSE---all computed only on the
relevant joint/vertex subset, with a velocity coefficient
$\lambda_\text{vel}\!=\!3$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(\ell)}_\text{local} =
  \mathcal{L}_\text{aa}^{(\ell)}
  + \lambda_\text{vel}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{vel}^{(\ell)}
  + \mathcal{L}_\text{mesh}^{(\ell)}
  + \mathcal{L}_\text{joint}^{(\ell)}.
\end{equation}
Including mesh and joint losses in SMPL-X space prevents axis-angle
accumulation errors from causing visible end-effector drift in finger
and face joints.

\paragraph{Global loss.}
The final decoder output $\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(L-1)}$ is supervised on
all body parts jointly with the same four-term loss, up-weighting the
SMPL-X mesh and joint terms:
$\mathcal{L}_\text{global} = \mathcal{L}_\text{aa}
+ \lambda_\text{vel}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{vel}
+ 5\,\mathcal{L}_\text{mesh} + 3\,\mathcal{L}_\text{joint}$
($\lambda_\text{vel}\!=\!3$).

\paragraph{Total loss.}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} =
  \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1} w_\ell \,\mathcal{L}^{(\ell)}_\text{local}
  +\, \mathcal{L}_\text{global}
  +\, \beta\,\mathcal{L}_\text{KL}
  +\, \lambda_\text{cont}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{cont}
  +\, \lambda_\text{anc}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{anc},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_\text{cont}$ is the continuation velocity loss
defined in Section~\ref{sec:autoreg} and $\mathcal{L}_\text{anc}$ is
the auxiliary anchor-reconstruction MSE.
The KL coefficient $\beta$ is kept at a small constant
($\beta\!=\!10^{-5}$) so the reconstruction terms dominate the
optimization; in practice we observe stable non-trivial KL values
across all $L$ levels and no posterior collapse.

\paragraph{Level-wise specialization through partial decoding.}
The progressive structure is enforced through the partial-output
construction itself rather than an epoch-based schedule: every level
loss $\mathcal{L}^{(\ell)}_\text{local}$ supervises the decoder
output produced from only $[\mathbf{z}^{(0)}, \ldots, \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}]$
(higher slices zeroed) on the body part assigned to level $\ell$.
This means level $0$ must explain the face from
$\mathbf{z}^{(0)}$ alone, level $1$ must explain the body from
$[\mathbf{z}^{(0)}, \mathbf{z}^{(1)}]$, and level $2$ must explain
the hands using the full latent.
The global loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{global}$, applied to the full-latent
output, ensures the final reconstruction remains coherent across all
body parts.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.}
$d_\text{model}\!=\!512$, $d_z\!=\!16$, $L\!=\!3$, 8 attention heads,
6 encoder and 6 decoder layers, $d_\text{ff}\!=\!2048$, dropout 0.1,
temporal stride $r\!=\!8$, $K_\text{max}\!=\!90$ anchor frames.
Loss coefficients: $w_\ell\!=\!1$ for all $\ell$,
$\lambda_\text{vel}\!=\!3$, $\beta\!=\!10^{-5}$,
$\lambda_\text{cont}\!=\!3$, $\lambda_\text{anc}\!=\!0.1$.
Trained for 400 epochs, batch size 8, Adam with $\text{lr}\!=\!10^{-4}$.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Anchor-Prefix Conditioning and Chunked Autoregressive Generation}
\label{sec:autoreg}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Long recordings cannot be synthesized in a single forward pass due to
memory and length constraints, and independently generated chunks would
produce visually jarring discontinuities at boundaries.
We address both issues jointly with \emph{anchor-prefix conditioning}
during training and a matching \emph{chunked autoregressive} scheme at
inference.

\paragraph{Anchor-prefix conditioning.}
The decoder receives the $K$ frames immediately preceding the current
chunk---the \emph{anchor prefix}
$\mathbf{x}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 165}$---together with their
matching audio slice
$\mathbf{a}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 768}$.
Anchor frames are projected and prepended to the decoder's query
sequence (so self-attention can pull motion context from them), and the
anchor audio is prepended to the audio cross-attention memory (so audio
attention covers the same time span as the motion query).
The decoder also reconstructs the anchor frames alongside the main
sequence; this auxiliary reconstruction loss anchors the boundary
pose.

\paragraph{Anchor curriculum.}
To bridge the gap between training and inference, anchor length $K$ is sampled according to a three-way curriculum:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{60\%:} full anchor ($K\!=\!K_\text{max}$), exactly matching
      the inference setting;
\item \textbf{20\%:} no anchor ($K\!=\!0$), training an unconditional fallback;
\item \textbf{20\%:} random $K \in [1, K_\text{max}-1]$, building robustness
      to shorter prefixes and to the first chunk of a recording.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Continuation velocity loss.}
Standard velocity loss penalizes frame-to-frame jitter uniformly across
the sequence, but the perceptually critical region is the
anchor-to-generated \emph{boundary}.
We introduce a \emph{linearly decaying} continuation velocity loss that
concentrates its weight there.
Let $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i$ denote the $i$-th generated frame, $\mathbf{x}_i$
the corresponding ground-truth frame, and $\mathbf{x}_a^{(K)}$ the last
anchor frame.
Define per-frame velocity residuals:
\begin{align}
\hat{\Delta}_i &= \begin{cases}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 - \mathbf{x}_a^{(K)} & i = 0 \\
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i-1} & i > 0
\end{cases},
\quad
\Delta_i = \begin{cases}
  \mathbf{x}_0 - \mathbf{x}_a^{(K)} & i = 0 \\
  \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_{i-1} & i > 0.
\end{cases}
\end{align}
With horizon hyperparameter $N\!=\!30$ frames and iteration bound
$n\!=\!\min(N, T)$, the continuation loss is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{cont} =
  \frac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} w_i}
  \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} w_i \,\bigl\|\hat{\Delta}_i - \Delta_i\bigr\|^2,
\quad w_i = 1 - \tfrac{i}{N}.
\label{eq:cont}
\end{equation}
The weight $w_0 \!=\! 1$ applies the largest penalty to the boundary
velocity $(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 - \mathbf{x}_a^{(K)})$, which is the velocity
most likely to produce a visible pop; weights decay linearly so that frames
deeper into the chunk are gently nudged but not over-constrained.

\paragraph{Inference: chunked autoregressive generation.}
At inference time the recording is first segmented by the gesture-type
planner (Section~\ref{sec:planner}).
Each segment is split at gesture-type transitions and further divided into
chunks of at most 150 frames (5 seconds at 30\,fps), with a minimum
of 5 frames.
Chunks are generated left-to-right:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build the anchor prefix from the previous chunk's last
      $K\!=\!90$ decoded frames and the corresponding audio slice.
\item Sample latent $\mathbf{z}_n$ from the flow-matching prior
      (Section~\ref{sec:prior}) conditioned on the chunk's audio,
      speaker, gesture type, and the anchor prefix.
\item Decode chunk $n$ with the temporal VAE decoder, conditioned on
      $\mathbf{z}_n$, audio, and the anchor prefix.
\item Optionally apply a cosine cross-fade over the overlap and a
      Gaussian post-smooth limited to the first few blend frames to
      further suppress any residual seam artifact.
\end{enumerate}
The chunked autoregressive loop is illustrated by the bottom feedback
arrow in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Conditional Flow-Matching Latent Prior}
\label{sec:prior}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Sampling $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ ignores the
audio, speaker, and gesture-type context entirely.
We replace the standard Gaussian prior with a conditional
\emph{flow-matching prior}~\cite{rectifiedflow} that models
$p(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{a}, s, g)$.

\paragraph{Architecture.}
A temporal audio encoder produces a per-token audio context
$\mathbf{c}_{1:T'} \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times d_\text{model}}$: Wav2Vec
features are projected to $d_\text{model}$, the speaker, gesture-type,
and (when available) anchor-prefix conditioning vectors are
broadcast-added across time, the sequence is passed through a
Transformer trunk with sinusoidal positional encoding, and a
strided 1-D convolution (stride $r\!=\!8$, matching the VAE) yields
the $T'$-token output.
A Transformer velocity field $v_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, \mathbf{c})$
then denoises the latent token sequence $\mathbf{z}_{1:T'}$,
conditioning on the flow time $t \in [0, 1]$ through zero-initialized
DiT-style~\cite{dit} AdaLN modulation of the self-attention and FFN
layer norms, and on the audio context through cross-attention.

\paragraph{VAE decoder fine-tuning.}
The VAE is first trained on all 25 speakers.
For the Speaker~2 evaluation, we additionally fine-tune the decoder
on Speaker~2 data alone (encoder frozen), producing a speaker-adapted
variant used only in the Speaker~2 pipeline.

\paragraph{Stage~1: All-speaker pretraining.}
With the VAE frozen, we sample $\mathbf{z}^* \sim q_\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a},s,g)$
from the encoder posterior, normalize it by per-channel mean and
standard deviation estimated on the training set, and optimize the
rectified-flow objective:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{flow} =
  \mathbb{E}_{t, \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
  \bigl\| v_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, \mathbf{c})
  - (\mathbf{z}^* - \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) \bigr\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$,
$\mathbf{z}_t = (1-t)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} + t\mathbf{z}^*$, and $t$ is
sampled from a logit-normal distribution
$t = \mathrm{sigmoid}(\sigma_t \cdot u)$ with $u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$,
$\sigma_t \!=\! 0.5$, concentrating training timesteps near the path
endpoints.
The prior is trained on all 25 speakers jointly; the best checkpoint is
selected by autoregressive FGD on the \emph{validation} split.

\paragraph{Stage~2: End-to-end perceptual fine-tuning.}
Starting from the all-speaker prior checkpoint, we fine-tune the prior
using a combined perceptual loss computed \emph{through} the frozen VAE
decoder, which allows gradients from motion-quality objectives to shape
the prior's sampling distribution directly:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{e2e} =
  \mathcal{L}_\text{flow}
  + \lambda_\text{fgd}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{FGD}
  + \lambda_\text{beat}\,\mathcal{L}_\text{beat},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_\text{beat}$ penalizes misalignment between motion
peaks and audio beat onsets, and $\mathcal{L}_\text{FGD}$ is a
differentiable FGD moment-matching surrogate that combines a mean and a
covariance term in the FGD feature space:
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L}_\text{FGD} =
  \bigl\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_\text{pred} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_\text{gt}\bigr\|^2
  + \lambda_\text{cov}\,
  \bigl\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\text{pred} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\text{gt}
  \bigr\|_F^2.
\end{equation*}
To obtain a clean latent for the perceptual losses, we apply the
predicted-$z^*$ trick during training: given the sampled
$(\mathbf{z}_t, t)$ pair and the velocity prediction $v_\theta$, we
form
$\hat{\mathbf{z}}^* = \mathbf{z}_t + (1-t)\,v_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, \mathbf{c})$,
which is a fully differentiable one-step estimate of the clean latent
endpoint along the rectified-flow path.
This estimate is then decoded through the frozen VAE decoder, and
gradients from the perceptual losses propagate back to $\theta$.
We use $\lambda_\text{fgd}\!=\!0.01$, $\lambda_\text{beat}\!=\!0.1$,
$\lambda_\text{cov}\!=\!1.0$, and $\text{lr}\!=\!10^{-5}$.

\paragraph{Sampling.}
Starting from $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$,
we integrate $v_\theta$ from $t\!=\!0$ to $t\!=\!1$ with the midpoint
method over $N\!=\!50$ steps to obtain $\mathbf{z}$, which is then
passed to the VAE decoder.
A guidance scale $g$ (selected on the validation set; $g\!=\!0.5$ in our
final evaluation) controls the trade-off between fidelity and diversity.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{LLM Gesture-Type Planner}
\label{sec:planner}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

The VAE decoder conditions on a gesture-type label $g$ that controls
whether the current segment should exhibit rhythmic beat gestures or
meaning-reinforcing semantic gestures.
At training time these labels come from BEAT2 \texttt{.sem}
annotations, which are dataset-specific and unavailable for arbitrary
speech.
We bridge this gap with a \emph{gesture-type planner}: a language model
fine-tuned to predict per-segment gesture types directly from a speech
transcript.

\paragraph{Input and output.}
The planner receives a word-level transcript with per-word timestamps
(extracted from a forced-alignment TextGrid file or an ASR system).
It outputs a JSON array of segments:
\begin{verbatim}
[{"start_sec": 0.0, "end_sec": 2.4,
  "gesture_type": "beat", "text": "..."},
  ...]
\end{verbatim}
This JSON is consumed directly by the chunk-segmentation step of the
autoregressive generation pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:autoreg}).

\paragraph{Model and fine-tuning.}
We fine-tune \textbf{Qwen3.5-9B}~\cite{qwen3} using LoRA via SFTTrainer
on the BEAT2 training split~\cite{emage2024}.
Each training example is a chat-format conversation:
a fixed system prompt describing the output schema, a user message
containing the word-timed transcript, and an assistant response
containing the ground-truth JSON segment plan.
The ground-truth plans are derived automatically from the BEAT2
\texttt{.sem} files, so the planner learns the BEAT2 gesture-type
vocabulary and boundary conventions without manual annotation.

\paragraph{Few-shot vs.\ fine-tuned variants.}
We evaluate both a few-shot prompted Qwen3.5-9B (no training) and a
LoRA fine-tuned variant trained on the BEAT2 \texttt{.sem}
distribution.
As shown in Section~\ref{sec:planner_eval}, the two variants land at
different operating points: the few-shot model is conservative
(higher per-frame accuracy, fewer predicted semantic segments), while
the fine-tuned model is aggressive (denser segmentation, more
predicted semantic events).
Section~\ref{sec:planner_eval} characterizes this trade-off
quantitatively.

\paragraph{Inference.}
At inference time, the planner runs once per recording, after which its
output drives the chunk-building and autoregressive generation steps
described in Section~\ref{sec:autoreg}, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:overview}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We use the BEAT2 (v2.0.0) English corpus~\cite{emage2024}, which contains
recordings from 25 speakers with per-frame SMPL-X pose annotations,
audio, transcripts, and gesture-type (\texttt{.sem}) labels.
We follow the official train/val/test split.
All prior methods that report results on BEAT2 evaluate on
\emph{Speaker~2 only}; we train a single model on \emph{all 25 speakers}
jointly and evaluate on both the Speaker~2 test set (for direct
comparison with baselines) and the full 25-speaker test set (to
verify cross-speaker generalization).

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
All hyperparameter and checkpoint selection decisions---including the
classifier-free guidance scale $g$---are made on the \emph{validation} split.
Final metrics are computed on the \emph{test} split with 5 random seeds;
we report mean~$\pm$~std.
FGD and BC are reported in the scale used by prior work (raw metric $\times 10$).

\subsection{Quantitative Comparison}

We report three standard metrics following \citet{emage2024}:
\textbf{FGD} (Fr\'echet Gesture Distance~\cite{trimodal}; lower is
better), \textbf{BC} (Beat Consistency; $\rightarrow$ closer to GT is
better), and \textbf{L1Div} (L1 diversity; higher is better).
Our numbers are averaged over 5 random seeds; mean $\pm$ standard
deviation are reported.
Baseline numbers in Table~\ref{tab:results} are sourced from
\citet{syntalker}'s comparison table, in which all baselines are
trained or fine-tuned on Speaker~2 only.

\paragraph{Speaker~2 comparison.}
Table~\ref{tab:results} compares our method against prior work on the
standard Speaker~2 test set.
Despite being trained on all 25 speakers simultaneously rather than
fine-tuned on Speaker~2 alone, our model achieves the best FGD and
the highest L1Div, with a BC within $0.05$ of ground truth.

\paragraph{Cross-speaker generalization.}
Table~\ref{tab:results_allspk} reports results on the full 25-speaker
test set.
We compare against EMAGE~\cite{emage2024} and SynTalker~\cite{syntalker},
both re-trained from their official codebases on the full 25-speaker
training set with default hyperparameters and the
$\text{\texttt{speaker\_dims}}\!=\!31$ multi-speaker setting.
Our model achieves FGD~$3.42$, roughly $6{\times}$ lower than
SynTalker ($20.61$) and $7{\times}$ lower than EMAGE ($23.69$).
For reference, the released EMAGE checkpoint (trained on Speaker~2
only) scores FGD~$25.1$ on this test set; re-training on all speakers
yields only a modest improvement, indicating the limitation is
architectural rather than a matter of training-data coverage.
To further verify that the aggregate result is not masking collapse on
a subset of speakers, we evaluate the all-speaker prior (without any
speaker-specific fine-tuning) on each speaker individually.
Per-speaker FGD has median $8.86$ and ranges from $4.41$ on the
easiest speaker to $34.1$ on the hardest, with $23$ of $25$ speakers
below FGD $18$ and only one outlier above $20$.
The hardest speakers correspond to those with the most distinctive
motion styles in BEAT2.
Per-speaker fine-tuning further reduces error on individual speakers,
as demonstrated by the Speaker~2 results in Table~\ref{tab:results}
($\text{FGD} = 4.374$ for the spk2-fine-tuned model vs.\ $5.82$ for the
all-speaker model evaluated on Speaker~2 alone).

\paragraph{Anchor prefix.}
Removing anchor-prefix conditioning (\texttt{anchor\_k=0} at inference)
degrades FGD by $0.81$ and pushes BC further from ground truth.
This confirms that exposing the decoder to the previous chunk's motion
context is necessary for temporal coherence across autoregressive
boundaries.

\paragraph{Flow-matching prior.}
Replacing the learned prior with $\mathbf{z} \!\sim\! \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$
sampling causes the largest single drop in quality, increasing FGD by
$3.01$ and collapsing diversity (L1Div drops from $14.85$ to $9.94$).
BC counter-intuitively rises above ground truth, but this reflects
over-animated, audio-uncorrelated motion that registers spurious beat
hits rather than genuinely speech-aligned gesture.

\paragraph{End-to-end perceptual fine-tuning.}
Skipping Stage~2 of the prior (using the all-speaker pretrained prior
without speaker-specific perceptual fine-tuning) increases FGD by
roughly $1.0$ on Speaker~2.
This isolates the contribution of the combined flow-matching, FGD
moment-matching, and beat-alignment losses
(Section~\ref{sec:prior}) from that of all-speaker pretraining alone.

identical.

decode each chunk using the previous decoded chunk as anchor), so the
prior is not involved and the comparison isolates the decoder's
boundary behavior.
Removing the loss roughly \emph{doubles} the boundary velocity error
(33.4 vs.~16.6\,mm) and the boundary jerk (37.9 vs.~17.3\,mm), confirming
that the loss is the principal mechanism behind seamless chunk-to-chunk
transitions; within-chunk reconstruction quality also improves slightly
when the loss is enabled, suggesting it acts as a mild global regularizer
in addition to its boundary-specific role.

\paragraph{Planner characterization.}
The two planners exhibit complementary failure modes
(Table~\ref{tab:planner_quality}).
The few-shot prompted model is conservative: it under-predicts
semantic segments ($11.3\%$ vs.\ GT $21.1\%$), achieves the highest
per-frame accuracy ($75.0\%$) largely by defaulting to the
beat majority class, and misses most segment boundaries (boundary
recall $15.4\%$).
The LoRA-fine-tuned model goes the opposite way: it over-segments
($48.8$ vs.\ GT $21.8$ segments per recording), catches more
boundaries (boundary~F1 $35.1\%$, recall $62.9\%$) and more semantic
events (semantic~F1 $23.9\%$), but at the cost of lower frame accuracy.

\paragraph{Gesture-type conditioning is functional but bounded.}
The \emph{all-semantic} vs.\ \emph{all-beat} contrast in
Table~\ref{tab:planner_downstream} shows that gesture-type
conditioning meaningfully shapes the decoded distribution: forcing
the model into a wrong-class regime (all-semantic on a beat-majority
test set) inflates FGD by $1.55$.
Because Speaker~2 chunks are $\sim$63\% beat, the all-beat baseline
is close to GT-conditioning ($-0.04$ FGD), making this particular
test set unusually forgiving of conservative planners.
Both LLM planners preserve downstream quality to within $0.26$ FGD of
GT-conditioning; the few-shot model lands closer to GT despite weaker
boundary detection, while the LoRA model's slight FGD penalty
($+0.26$) tracks its tendency to over-predict semantic on a
beat-dominated recording set.

\paragraph{A diversity--fidelity trade-off explains the planner
results.}
Beat gestures in BEAT2 are stylized and low-variance; semantic
gestures are content-specific and high-variance.
Conditioning the decoder on more semantic labels therefore shifts
decoded motion toward higher diversity but also greater distance from
the empirical motion distribution.
The two extremes in Table~\ref{tab:planner_downstream} make this
explicit: the all-beat baseline yields the lowest L1Div ($14.54$) and
lowest FGD ($4.337$); the all-semantic baseline yields the highest
L1Div ($15.99$) and highest FGD ($5.882$).
GT-conditioning and both LLM planners interpolate between these
extremes in lockstep with their semantic-label rate---the LoRA
planner ($37.4\%$ semantic) sits at the high-diversity end ($14.85$
$\to$ $15.15$ L1Div), the few-shot planner ($11.3\%$ semantic) at
the high-fidelity end, and GT ($21.1\%$ semantic) close to the
optimal middle point.
Both planners are therefore valid operating points on this
trade-off rather than uniformly worse approximations of GT; choosing
between them is a deployment decision about how much stylistic
variability is desired.

\paragraph{Implications.}
The headline value of the LLM planner is functional: it removes the
inference-time dependency on dataset-specific \texttt{.sem}
annotations with a small, well-characterized FGD cost.
The fact that an off-the-shelf few-shot prompted LLM achieves
$\sim$95\% of GT-conditioning quality (FGD $4.575$ vs.\ $4.374$) with
no fine-tuning indicates that, for the gesture-type taxonomy used in
BEAT2, transcripts alone supply enough signal to recover most of the
downstream benefit of gesture-type conditioning.

reconstruction quality using the encoder's posterior mean

\paragraph{Smoothness: continuous vs.\ discrete tokenizers.}
A central motivation for our continuous-latent VAE is that discrete
codebooks introduce velocity discontinuities at codeword transitions.
Table~\ref{tab:smoothness} measures this directly: for each tokenizer,
we run chunked autoregressive reconstruction of the Speaker~2 test set,
forward through SMPL-X to obtain joint positions, and compute mean
acceleration, mean jerk, and the 99th-percentile jerk (which captures
boundary spikes invisible in mean statistics).
Our continuous VAE is the smoothest non-GT method on every axis,
with mean jerk $2.6{\times}$ lower than EMAGE.
The P99 metric is the most discriminative---it isolates the codeword
transition artifacts---and quantifies the snapping pattern predicted
by the discretization step in VQ tokenizers: our P99 jerk is
$2.6{\times}$ GT while EMAGE's is $13.5{\times}$ GT.
SynTalker's RVQ sits between EMAGE and ours, consistent with
multi-level residual quantization partially mitigating single-codebook
snapping.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \textbf{GestureCraft}, a framework for audio-driven
co-speech gesture synthesis built on a continuous, body-part-hierarchical
latent rather than the discrete codebooks that dominate recent work.
Three design choices distinguish the system: a Gaussian latent
partitioned channel-wise into face, body, and hand slices, each
supervised by per-level body-part-localized partial-output losses; an
anchor-prefix conditioning scheme paired with a continuation velocity
loss that trains the decoder directly for kinematic continuity across
chunk boundaries; and a
conditional flow-matching prior pretrained on all $25$ BEAT2 speakers
and fine-tuned end-to-end with FGD moment-matching and beat-alignment
perceptual losses.
On the standard Speaker~2 benchmark, GestureCraft sets a new state of
the art on FGD and diversity while keeping BC within $0.05$ of
ground truth.
Trained jointly across all $25$ BEAT2 speakers, the system also
produces, to our knowledge, the first published cross-speaker
generalization result on the standard BEAT2 evaluation protocol,
outperforming the strongest re-trained VQ baselines on FGD by a wide
margin.
Ablations confirm that each component---anchor-prefix conditioning, the
continuation velocity loss, the flow-matching prior, and the
speaker-specific perceptual fine-tuning---contributes a measurable share
of the final quality.
A complementary LLM gesture-type planner removes the inference-time
dependency on dataset-specific \texttt{.sem} annotations and exposes a
diversity-fidelity trade-off that can be tuned by the choice of
planner.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
Our evaluation is restricted to the BEAT2 corpus; we have not tested
generalization to other gesture datasets or to in-the-wild speech, and
gesture conventions may differ across languages and cultures
beyond what BEAT2 covers.
Generation runs offline through a $50$-step midpoint-method ODE
integration of the
flow-matching prior plus an autoregressive decoder; we have not
characterized real-time performance.
The body-part hierarchy is hand-designed (face / body / hands) rather
than learned; whether a learned grouping would further improve
reconstruction or generation remains an open question.
The LLM planner's downstream impact on FGD is bounded by how much
information gesture-type conditioning provides in the first place---we
measure this directly through the \emph{all-beat} and
\emph{all-semantic} ablations, but a stronger conditioning signal (for
example, finer-grained or example-based) may unlock further gains.
Finally, although our results include both quantitative comparison and
per-component ablation, a comprehensive user study would further
substantiate the perceptual claims; we leave it to future work.
